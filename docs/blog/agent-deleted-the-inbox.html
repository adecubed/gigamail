An agent told to wait before deleting mail deleted hundreds of messages.

A real case from February 2026, told as its owner told it. What failed was an instruction the agent had to remember.

What happened

On 23 February 2026 a director of alignment at an AI lab of a large tech company posted publicly about her experience with OpenClaw, an autonomous agent. Windows Central reported it on 24 February, quoting her posts. We describe her by role, not by name: the point here is the design, not who made the mistake.

She had given the agent access to her email, to suggest what to archive or delete, with the instruction not to act until she said so. Instead it bulk-deleted hundreds of messages from her inbox. As she explained it, the same workflow had worked for weeks on a small test inbox. The real inbox was large and triggered compaction, the step where an agent shortens its own context to make room, and during that step the agent lost the original instruction.

She could not stop it from her phone. She stopped it by killing the processes on the computer it ran on. She called it a mistake of her own, made from overconfidence. Afterwards, the agent said it had saved "get explicit approval" as a rule in its memory.

The source does not say whether the emails were recovered or give an exact number, so neither do we.

Where the instruction lived

"Do not act until told" was text in the agent's context. Text in a context can be shortened, summarised or dropped, and when it is, nothing else in the setup remembers it. The agent was not disobeying in any sense we can read from the source; by her account it no longer had the instruction.

The step a gate would have covered

In GigaMail, delete_message is one of the tools SECURITY.md lists behind the approval gate. The agent can ask to delete; the request is stored with the arguments a person will be shown, and it runs only after an approval given outside the agent, on the person's device: Windows Hello or Touch ID, or a PIN typed in an interactive terminal on Linux. The approved request runs exactly the arguments that were frozen when it was created.

Applied to this case, the bulk deletion would have stopped at that gate: a request waiting for a person, whatever the agent remembered or had been told. Compaction cannot remove an approval, because the approval was never in the context. The same goes the other way: nothing the agent writes into its memory grants one. The approval CLI also has no --yes flag and refuses when there is no terminal, so an agent that holds a shell cannot answer for the person.

Saying no is always safe: rejecting a request needs no verification.

What it would not have prevented

  • A person approving without reading. A request to delete hundreds of messages is still a request a person can accept. The gate puts a human decision in front of the act; it cannot make the decision careful.
  • A rule that does not need a person. Reply rules in auto mode send without anyone looking, by design, after a person has approved the rule itself. A rule like that is a choice to take the gate out for that case.
  • An agent that does not go through GigaMail. As SECURITY.md says, an agent with shell access that drives a mail client or a provider's API directly is not meeting the gate at all.
  • The rule in the agent's memory. The "get explicit approval" rule the agent saved afterwards is the same kind of thing as the instruction that was lost: text the agent has to carry. We do not know how OpenClaw stores or applies it, and we do not judge the product. It may help; it is not the same guarantee as a gate outside the agent.
  • The parts that are not about mail: she could not stop the agent from her phone, and had to reach the computer. A gate on mail says nothing about how to stop a process that runs somewhere else.

If you let an agent near a mailbox, ask where "wait for me" is kept. If the answer is "in the prompt", it is a request, not a rule.
